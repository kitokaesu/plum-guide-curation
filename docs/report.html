Plum Guide

Curation, inspections and bookings for the top 3% of homes

A full-stack demonstration of the operational platform behind a curated holiday-home brand: specialist Plum Test inspections, host availability and pricing, atomic guest bookings, self-service cancellations and four role-based portals.

Next.js 16 · React 19 · Prisma 6 · Cloudflare D1 Prices in EUR Demonstration build

Abstract. This report documents the research, design, implementation and testing of a curated home-rental platform modelled on Plum Guide. It explains why the company’s quality promise creates an operational problem, how the problem was translated into requirements for four user groups, how the database enforces the non-negotiable rules, and what was learned from exercising the finished system. It also records the free AI tools used during the build and separates what the system does well from what it deliberately does not do.

01

Introduction and scope

Plum Guide is a real company that curates holiday homes and claims to admit only “the top 3%” in each destination. The brand promise is simple to state and difficult to operate: every home must be visited, scored, approved and kept available; every booking must be confirmed without ever selling the same night twice; every cancellation must apply a published policy and release the dates immediately.

This project builds that operating model as a working web application. It is not a visual mock-up. A guest can search a real collection, select nights on a live calendar, create a reservation with an authoritative server-calculated total, print an invoice and cancel it. A host can submit a home, book a specialist inspection, close nights and set custom prices. A specialist can publish inspection slots and record a score that automatically approves or rejects a home. An administrator can see the whole pipeline, override an inspection decision and adjust a refund within the amount paid.

The report describes the work in the order it was carried out: research, requirements, technology selection, data modelling, interface design and iteration, AI-assisted implementation, testing and reflection. The companion artefacts are the presentation (docs/presentation.html) and the user guide (docs/user-guide.html).

Scope boundaryThis is a demonstration, not a production marketplace. Payments and confirmation emails are simulated, photography is curated stock imagery, and the seeded accounts use public demo passwords. Those choices are listed again in section 9 rather than hidden.

02

Company, sector and research

2.1 The chosen company

Plum Guide is a British vacation-rental company headquartered in London. It launched in 2016 and was founded by Doron Meyassed, Imran Arshed, Arif Meherali and Alex Lee. Public profiles describe offices in New York and Hong Kong and, by 2021, homes in more than 500 destinations across 29 countries. The company positions itself as a quality benchmark rather than a listing marketplace: its own story compares the Plum Guide to a Michelin star for restaurants, and says it has put more than 500,000 homes through its vetting process since launch [1][2].

The selection process combines data with human judgement. Plum Guide says its experts filter the available rentals, and independent “home critics” visit the homes that survive the initial screen. The inspection standard is commonly described as more than 150 criteria — from shower pressure and Wi-Fi speed to smell and kitchen equipment [1][4]. Only a small fraction of homes are accepted; press coverage has used both “top 1%” and “top 3%” depending on the year and the destination, which is itself a useful lesson about marketing figures [2][3][5].

The commercial model is a curated two-sided marketplace. Guests pay to stay in inspected homes; hosts gain access to guests who value the vetting; Plum Guide coordinates the curation, inspection and booking experience in between. The demonstration in this project focuses on that middle layer, not on replicating the public brand or its inventory.

2.2 The sector and why it is interesting

Short-term rentals are abundant and uneven. The official Plum Guide story opens with “15,000,000+ vacation homes listed online” and argues that choice has outgrown confidence [1]. Reviews help, but they are written by strangers with different standards, and a five-star average can hide a broken shower or a misleading location. The sector’s central problem is therefore not supply; it is trust at the point of decision.

Three business models compete in this space. Airbnb and Vrbo optimise for breadth and self-service. onefinestay and similar operators optimise for managed quality and higher-touch service. Plum Guide sits between them: a curated collection with an editorial voice, a human inspection and a booking platform. Its differentiation is credible only if the operational system behind it is trustworthy. A beautiful landing page cannot compensate for a double-booked night or a refund that never arrives.

2.3 Research conducted

The research was desk-based and deliberately limited to public sources, because no access to Plum Guide’s internal systems was available or appropriate. It had four parts:

  1. Company and proposition. The official “Our story” page supplied the mission, founder narrative, “top 3%” language and the inspection and care promises [1]. Wikipedia supplied a dated company profile, founding details, markets and funding history, with citations to contemporaneous press [2].
  2. Independent context. TechCrunch’s 2019 funding report framed the company as “vacation homes for the elite” [3]. Business Insider’s 2019 feature described the “1 in 100” acceptance rate and the Michelin Guide analogy [5]. PhocusWire’s startup profile described a test of more than 150 criteria [4].
  3. Current public experience. The live Plum Guide site was reviewed for information architecture: how homes are presented, how the host proposition is framed, and which trust signals are repeated at the point of booking [6].
  4. Technical research. Official documentation for Next.js, Prisma, Cloudflare D1 and Cloudflare Workers was used to choose an architecture that could actually run the transactional rules (section 4) [7][8][9][10].

The research produced a clear design implication: the brand promise needs explicit, auditable states. “Beautiful” is a judgement; “approved after an inspection score of 85 or more” is a rule that can be implemented, tested and explained to a host. The application therefore turns the marketing promise into a status machine and a set of financial policies.

Research limitationsPublic sources disagree on whether the company accepts the top 1% or top 3%, and figures change over time. This report treats them as brand context, not as verified operational data. No interviews with Plum Guide staff, hosts or guests were possible; the user needs in section 3 are derived from the public proposition and from standard marketplace workflows.

03

Problem, users and envisaged functionalities

3.1 The problem being investigated

How can a curated rental brand prove its quality promise and operate it reliably, when curation, inspection, availability, pricing, booking and refunds all affect the same home?

That question breaks into five concrete operational problems:

  • Evidence. Guests need to see why a home is in the collection, not just that it is. The inspection score and notes must be visible and tied to a real visit.
  • State. A home is not simply “listed” or “unlisted”. It is a draft, awaiting inspection, approved or rejected. Each state has different permissions and different visibility.
  • Concurrency. Two guests can choose the same dates at the same moment. A polite error is not enough; the database must make a double sale impossible.
  • Money and policy. The total, the service fee and the refund must be calculated by the server, stored with the booking and applied consistently. A refund must also release the calendar.
  • Role separation. Hosts, specialists and administrators need different views of the same facts. Every mutation must re-check who is allowed to perform it.

3.3 Envisaged functionalities

The requirements were grouped into the flows a user can actually complete, rather than a feature list disconnected from screens:

Public and guest

  • Editorial landing page with the Plum Test explanation and featured homes.
  • Approved-only collection with text search and three sort orders.
  • Property detail with inspection score, host, capacity, description and live calendar.
  • Live quote: nightly subtotal, €180 cleaning, 8% service fee, total.
  • Instant booking with a human-readable reference; confirmation and printable invoice.
  • Guest dashboard with upcoming and past trips, spend, invoice and cancellation.
  • Refund preview with policy tier before a cancellation is confirmed.

Host, specialist and admin

  • Host listing creation (draft) and editing of title and base rate.
  • Specialist slot booking for the Plum Test; no self-created host slots.
  • 365-day availability manager with open/closed nights and custom prices.
  • Specialist slot publishing/withdrawal and an inspection audit form.
  • Automatic approval at a score of 85 or above; referral below it.
  • Admin review queue, approve/reject override and refund override.
  • Admin metrics: GMV, approved homes, pipeline, cancellation rate, refunds, members.

3.4 Non-functional requirements

  • Correctness under concurrency: no double booking, no double refund, no partial cancellation.
  • Security: hashed passwords, signed HTTP-only sessions, server-side authorisation and ownership checks on every mutation.
  • Consistency of money: a single EUR formatter and server-calculated totals; no client-supplied amounts trusted.
  • Accessibility: labelled controls, keyboard-operable dialog with focus trap and Escape, visible focus, live regions for quote and error updates, skip link.
  • Responsiveness: usable at phone width; tables scroll rather than break the layout.
  • Operability: one-command local start, idempotent database bootstrap, deployable Worker build.

Out of scope for this version: real card payments, transactional email, photo uploads, external calendar sync, multi-language content, tax/VAT handling, native apps.

4.2 Why this architecture, and what it costs

The decisive constraint was the runtime, not the UI. D1 is a SQLite database exposed through a Worker binding. It does not support interactive transactions, and Prisma’s D1 adapter executes statements individually rather than wrapping them in a transaction. A conventional booking implementation — read availability, then insert — leaves a window in which two requests can both read “free” and both write. The application therefore uses D1’s batch(), which executes its statements atomically, and pushes the decisive rules into the schema: a unique night ledger, a unique cancellation per booking, and a trigger that refuses to insert a night the host has closed.

This is a deliberate trade-off. The booking code contains raw SQL instead of a portable Prisma transaction, which makes a future move to PostgreSQL a real migration rather than a configuration change. In exchange, the current deployment is correct on the platform it actually runs on. The report returns to this in section 8.

Server components handle read-heavy pages: the landing page, collection, property detail, confirmation, invoice and all four dashboards. Client components are limited to calendars, the cancellation dialog, listing forms, the availability editor, the inspection form, moderation controls and the refund override. Every mutation goes through a route handler that re-establishes the session, checks role and ownership, validates input and recomputes money. The browser is never trusted for identity, ownership or totals.

4.3 Runtime environment

Local development

npm run dev starts Next.js on 0.0.0.0:3000. Wrangler provides a local D1 database from freebuff.wrangler.jsonc. On the first database access, the Worker creates the schema, indexes, trigger and demo rows, then writes a version marker in plum_meta. Subsequent cold starts perform one indexed read. Local data persists in .wrangler/state.

Deployed runtime

OpenNext builds a Cloudflare Worker from the Next.js app. Freebuff Sites provides the D1 binding named DB and injects AUTH_SECRET as a platform secret. The same bootstrap runs on first request. The architecture deliberately avoids Node-only APIs, native database drivers and long-running background processes.

Environment configurationThe .env file is used by the Prisma CLI and the seed generator, not by the runtime. The runtime reads the D1 binding. AUTH_SECRET must be a long random value in production; local development may use the built-in fallback. It is never exposed in a NEXT_PUBLIC_* variable.

4.4 Alternatives considered

  • PostgreSQL + Prisma transactions on a Node host. Simpler transaction semantics and a more portable schema, but requires a managed database and a long-running Node server; it would not demonstrate the edge deployment that the rest of the stack targets.
  • Supabase/Firebase-style backend. Fast for CRUD, but the booking rules would still need a transactional function or database constraint. The project’s educational point is precisely the constraint design, so a SQL database with explicit keys was preferable.
  • A client-only prototype with local storage. Would make the interface easy but would not test concurrency, authorisation or money handling — the three areas where this domain is actually difficult.

Database structure and relationships

The relational model has seven business tables plus a small bootstrap marker. It separates intent (the host calendar), commitments (bookings and their night ledger), evidence (inspection slots) and exceptions (cancellations). This separation is what makes the rules auditable.

5.1 Table catalogue

5.3 Rules encoded in the schema and domain

  • Approval: a specialist score of 85 or above sets the property to APPROVED; below 85 sets REJECTED and sends it to the admin queue. Admins may override either outcome.
  • Availability: a night is available only if the host has not closed it and no confirmed booking holds it. The booking calendar projects both sources; the host calendar marks reserved nights separately.
  • Booking: dates must be valid, at least one night and at most 30; check-in cannot be in the past; the property must still be APPROVED. Pricing uses the custom price per night when present, otherwise the base rate, plus €180 cleaning and 8% service.
  • Night ledger: the unique (property_id, date) index plus the booking_nights_closed_guard trigger make the insert fail rather than create an inconsistent booking. The whole batch rolls back on either conflict.
  • Cancellation: only a CONFIRMED booking can be cancelled; the refund follows the check-in window; the cancellation, night deletion and calendar release commit in one batch.
  • Admin override: a refund may be adjusted up to the booking total and is marked admin_override = true; it never exceeds what the guest paid.

5.4 Data lifecycle and seed

The authoring dataset is generated in prisma/seed.mjs against a local SQLite file, then exported to SQL statements in src/lib/d1-seed-sql.ts. The D1 database is empty in a new environment, and the platform does not expose arbitrary SQL to the deployer, so src/lib/d1-bootstrap.ts creates the schema and demo data on first use. Every statement is CREATE … IF NOT EXISTS or INSERT OR IGNORE, which makes concurrent cold starts harmless. A version marker in plum_meta short-circuits later boots.

The seed contains 9 users, 13 homes (9 approved, 2 awaiting inspection, 1 rejected, 1 draft), 7 bookings including two cancellations, 150 calendar days per home and 21 inspection slots in completed, available and booked states. It is anchored to the day it is generated so the demo always has future dates. The seed’s variety is intentional: every status and every role can be demonstrated without editing the database.

Schema change procedureUpdate prisma/schema.prisma, mirror the DDL in d1-bootstrap.ts, bump SEED_VERSION, regenerate the Prisma client and re-export the seed. The committed SQLite migrations support the authoring database, not the deployed D1 database.

06

Interface decisions and improvements

6.1 Design direction

The interface takes its cues from editorial hospitality rather than SaaS dashboards. The palette is warm cream, charcoal ink and antique gold, with moss, amber and wine reserved for approval, in-progress and destructive states. Headings use Fraunces, a display serif; interface text uses Inter. The intent is that the public site feels like a travel publication and the portals feel like a private concierge desk, while sharing one visual language.

The most important interface decision was to make evidence visible at the point of decision: every property card shows the Plum score; the detail page repeats it next to the inspection context; the booking panel shows the full quote; the cancellation dialog shows the refund before the destructive button. Trust is not a banner — it is the presence of the facts needed to act.

Role-aware navigation keeps each portal focused: a host sees “Overview”, “Add a home” and “Explore homes”; a specialist sees “Inspections” and “The collection”; an admin sees “Operations”. The dashboard layout enforces authentication once, and each page enforces its role. Empty states explain the next action rather than leaving a blank table.

6.3 Accessibility

Accessibility was treated as part of the interface rather than a final checklist. The implementation includes a skip link and landmarks; labelled form fields; error regions with role="alert"; aria-live updates for the booking quote; aria-pressed on calendar dates; a modal with a focus trap, Escape handling and focus restoration; visible focus rings; and text contrast chosen for WCAG AA. Status is never conveyed by colour alone — badges carry words as well as tone. A full automated and assistive-technology audit is still recommended before a public launch; this project did not claim to have completed one.

6.4 Currency change

The application now presents every monetary value in euros. The change was made at the presentation boundary: a single eur() helper wraps Intl.NumberFormat with currency: "EUR", and all pages use it. A slim announcement banner above the header states “Now in euros” and explains that nightly rates, fees and refunds are in EUR. The footer repeats “Prices in euros (EUR)”. The README’s pricing formula and the field labels (“Base nightly rate in euros”) were aligned.

Amounts were re-denominated, not converted at an exchange rate. The seed and host-entered rates are authored values; the application does not call an FX service. That is appropriate for a demonstration and is stated explicitly in the limitations. A production system would need a rate source, a rounding policy, historical rate storage and tax treatment per jurisdiction.

7.2 Examples of contribution

  1. Schema and D1 bootstrap. The agent drafted the seven-table Prisma schema, the matching CREATE TABLE statements and the idempotent seed loader. The draft assumed native enums and Prisma interactive transactions; these were rejected after checking SQLite and D1 documentation, and replaced with validated strings and an atomic batch.
  2. Booking correctness. Given the concurrency requirement, the agent proposed the night-ledger design: one row per reserved night, a unique (property_id, date) index, a closed-night trigger and a single db.batch(). The design was then tested with two simultaneous requests; the result (one 201, one 409, one row) is recorded in section 8.
  3. Cancellation and refunds. The agent generated the refund tiers, the cancellation dialog and the batch that updates the booking, records the refund, deletes the night ledger and reopens the calendar. The policy was checked against the brief and the numbers were verified by hand.
  4. Seed data. A realistic dataset of nine users, thirteen homes, seven bookings, 150 days per home and 21 slots was generated in prisma/seed.mjs, then reviewed for date coherence and exported to D1 SQL.
  5. Interface and accessibility review. The agent identified missing focus management in the cancellation dialog and added a focus trap, Escape handling and focus restoration; it also added aria-live for the quote and explicit empty states.
  6. Debugging the screenshot pipeline. Captures occasionally submitted a native form before React hydrated. The agent added a hydration wait and retry logic to the Playwright script, producing repeatable screenshots for the guide.
  7. Documentation. Drafts of this report, the user guide and the slide deck were generated from the codebase and then edited for accuracy, tone and consistency with the actual UI labels.

08

Testing, results, difficulties and solutions

8.1 Test approach

Testing was black-box and integration-focused. The application was run locally against a fresh D1 database and exercised over HTTP, with the browser used for visual and interaction checks. The priorities were the places where a demo can look correct but behave incorrectly: concurrency, authorisation boundaries, money and date handling, the inspection state machine, and the cancellation/refund batch. There is no automated test suite in the repository yet; that is an honest limitation, not a claim of coverage.

Two kinds of evidence are distinguished below. Build-time tests were executed over HTTP against the running app and repeated in local workerd with a fresh D1 database, as recorded in the project build log. Documentation-pass checks were run for this revision and are limited to static verification and screenshot inspection; the full HTTP suite was not re-executed because the documentation environment did not expose a Node.js toolchain. The distinction matters: a green table row is not a substitute for a test that was actually run in this session.

8.3 Difficulties and solutions

DifficultyWhy it occurredSolutionResidual risk
D1 has no interactive transactionsSQLite edge database with a batch API; Prisma’s D1 adapter executes statements individually.One atomic db.batch(); correctness enforced by a unique night index, a unique cancellation and a closed-night trigger.Raw SQL is less portable; a move to Postgres would require rewriting these paths as transactions.
Double-booking raceA read-then-write flow leaves a gap between checking availability and inserting.The insert is the claim; the unique index rejects the loser and the whole batch rolls back.None observed; the guarantee is database-level.
Partial cancellationUpdating a booking, inserting a refund and reopening dates are separate facts.All four statements execute in one batch; the unique cancellation index prevents a second refund.A future payment-provider integration must be idempotent and reconciled with the DB record.
Date driftJavaScript dates and UTC formatting can move a calendar day by one across time zones.Date-only values are stored at UTC midnight; utilities parse and format ISO calendar dates rather than local timestamps.None observed in the tested time zone; a broader device matrix would help.
No native enums in SQLitePrisma cannot express a native enum for this provider.Status strings centralised in src/lib/constants.ts and validated at every boundary.A direct SQL write outside the app could still insert an invalid status.

09

Functionality, limitations and improvements

9.1 Functionalities implemented

Guest and public

  • Editorial landing page with Plum Test explanation and featured approved homes.
  • Approved-only collection with title/location search and three sort orders.
  • Property detail with score, host, capacity, description and live pricing calendar.
  • Server-priced booking with reference, confirmation and printable invoice.
  • Guest dashboard with upcoming/past trips, spend, invoice links and cancellation.
  • Refund preview, policy tiers, reason capture and automatic date release.
  • Consistent EUR presentation and an announcement banner.

Host, specialist and admin

  • Host listing submission, title/base-rate editing and portfolio metrics.
  • 365-day calendar with open/closed nights, custom prices and reserved-night protection.
  • Plum Test scheduling against specialist-published slots.
  • Specialist slot publishing/withdrawal and an audit form with notes and score.
  • Automatic approval at 85+, referral below, admin override either way.
  • Admin review queue, booking monitor, refund override with cap and audit label.
  • Role-based authentication, session cookies and ownership checks on every mutation.
  • Idempotent D1 bootstrap and a deployable Workers build.

9.2 Existing limitations

  • Simulated payments and email. No card is charged, no confirmation email is sent, and refunds are database records rather than money movements.
  • Print-based invoice. “Download invoice” opens the browser print dialog; there is no server-generated PDF.
  • Stock photography. Hosts choose from curated Unsplash images; there is no upload, moderation or image optimisation pipeline.
  • No background jobs. Reminders, payout reconciliation and scheduled status transitions are out of scope.
  • Demo credentials. Seeded accounts share simple public passwords; specialist/admin accounts are not self-service.
  • No automated tests or rate limiting. The HTTP test matrix is manual; auth and booking endpoints are not throttled.
  • Currency is display-level. EUR is the only presentation currency and amounts are re-denominated, with no FX source, historical rates or VAT engine.
  • Accessibility is not fully audited. Keyboard and semantic support are implemented, but no assistive-technology or WCAG conformance audit was completed.
  • Published preview may lag. The deployed preview was built before the currency change; the local build is the current source of truth.

9.3 Possible improvements

  1. Payments: Stripe Payment Intents, webhook-driven confirmation and idempotent refunds reconciled with the cancellation record.
  2. Email: transactional messages for confirmation, cancellation, inspection results and host reminders.
  3. Media: direct-to-R2 uploads with image validation, responsive variants and a moderation step before publication.
  4. Calendar sync: iCal import/export and two-way sync with Airbnb/Vrbo to prevent external double bookings.
  5. Quality programme: structured inspection checklists instead of a single score, photo evidence and re-inspection scheduling.
  6. Operational tooling: audit log for admin overrides, CSV exports, payout statements and dispute notes.
  7. Testing and hardening: Playwright end-to-end tests in CI, including a parallel booking test; rate limiting and bot protection on auth and booking routes.
  8. Localisation and tax: selectable display currency backed by an explicit FX source, VAT rules per jurisdiction and compliant invoices.
  9. Accessibility: WCAG 2.2 AA audit, screen-reader testing, reduced-motion support and automated axe checks in CI.

10

Conclusion

The project turns a marketing promise into an operating system. Plum Guide’s “top 3%” claim becomes a status machine with an auditable score; the booking promise becomes a unique night ledger and an atomic batch; the refund policy becomes a calculated record that releases the calendar in the same transaction. Four portals present the same facts to the four people who need them, with permissions that are checked on the server rather than implied by the interface.

The most valuable lesson was that the hard part of this domain is not the visual design. It is the interaction between a database constraint, a user’s expectation and a policy. A friendly error message is useful; a schema that cannot represent the bad state is better. The second lesson was about scope: a demonstration should be honest about what it simulates, and that honesty makes the implemented parts more credible, not less.

The build is a solid foundation rather than a finished commercial product. Payments, email, media, external calendars and a full accessibility audit are the next layer. The current system is intentionally small enough to explain end to end, and complete enough that every claim in this report can be demonstrated in the running application.
